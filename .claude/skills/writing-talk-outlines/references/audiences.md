# Audience profiles

Pick one in the brief. The profile sets what is assumed, what is explained, and how long a
slide gets.

## Mentor update

Two supervisors who know the project and saw the previous deck. They want: what changed
since last time, which published numbers moved and why, what is running, what the next
deliverable is, and decisions they are being asked to make. They remember the questions
they asked last time; answer those first. Twenty to thirty minutes, six to ten main slides.
No method exposition. Retractions stated plainly on the slide where the old number was.

## Group seminar

Physicists outside reactor physics. They read Langevin equations, Green's functions and
point processes natively and do not know what a Rossi-alpha measurement is. Teach, do not
defend. Every concept introduced must be used again later in the talk. Forty-five minutes,
about thirty slides, with timing checkpoints in the outline. Delayed neutrons get two
bullets because someone will ask; adjoint anything is out.

## Expert talk

One senior Monte Carlo or reactor-physics expert, possibly a future collaborator, plus the
mentors. Knows the method and the paper, has never used OpenMC. Interrupt-and-steer
format. A colleague walking a colleague through the pipeline: every claim provable from the
repo, every soft number with its error and its mechanism. One hour, eight main slides at
four to five minutes each, backups that answer predictable questions with a figure.

## Conference talk

Mixed specialist audience, strict clock. One claim for the whole talk, stated on slide two
and again at the end. Twelve to fifteen minutes, ten to twelve slides, one slide per
minute. Methods compressed to what is needed to judge the result. Finish a minute early.
